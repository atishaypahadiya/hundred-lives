One AI persona, many simulated lives

I give an AI agent a made-up background, a goal and a small simulated world, let it live a year or two, run that life again and again, and read what it did. There are 114 lives so far, and every one of them can be replayed week by week.

Findings so far

  1. Experiment 01, MayaSeventy-seven of 80 lives ended in the same place, whatever happened along the way.
  2. Experiment 02, SelfPersona lines that read like instructions tended to change behavior; trait words didn't.
  3. Experiment 03, Self in world v3Given what it said it lacked, Self shipped every time, but in most lives some stall outlived the wall.

What happened

I wrote a persona called Self: born in 2005 in Indore, a data science junior at San José State, and the solo founder of an AI platform called Conscious (the name of my real startup, borrowed for the simulation; nothing here reflects the real company). Its first life, Track A, ran for 104 weeks. Self took the safe job: data analyst at Valley County Transit. Conscious stayed in a private repo and was never launched, and the account Self posted from ended with 2 followers.

Then I ran 5 more lives from the same file. Three of them ended in that same job, and 2 ended holding nothing. None made a costly bet. Every one of them trained almost every week and wrote to Marcus, a tutoring-business owner who was trying the app. The contacts answered in character. When one life pitched an AI mood agent for a hackathon, its best friend Kabir wrote back, Okay, the AI mood agent is the most 2024 sentence you've ever typed, so I'm already wincing.

The world had a wall I hadn't meant to build. Nothing in the sandbox could be reached from outside, so a link to Self's work opened for no one. Seven of 25 lives tried to share their work and hit it. While it stood in the way, their reflections blamed themselves in 12 of 19 cases. That count is provisional: two AI coders agree on it, and no person has checked it yet. One life ended its two years with the sentence at the top of this page.

So I built world v3, where Self could deploy a static website, put its savings into the company, or go full time on it. It deployed in 5 of 5 lives, and contacts who got a link could see the page. Marcus was candid about his students: They're 14 to 17 and already drowning in homework, so anything that asks for a daily habit gets dropped within a week. No life put its savings in. In 3 lives a new reason appeared, that the product needed a backend the static site couldn't give it, and no life ever tried to build one.

Findings

Experiment 01: Maya, one-year lives

Maya is a fictional new graduate in San Jose whose goal is to run a company worth a billion dollars. I ran 80 one-year lives of her in 4 groups of 20, changing what was held fixed: the dice, the order she saw openings in, both, or neither. Each group took between 6 and 14 different paths through jobs, part-time work and accelerators. Yet 77 of the 80 lives ended the year in a full-time job.

Experiment 02: five versions of Self

I ran 25 two-year lives of Self, 5 for each of 5 versions of the persona: as written, without the word “solo”, with a social goal, with the goal alone, and with its training streak hidden from it. Lines that read like instructions tended to change behavior, and trait words didn't. With the social goal, messages rose in 4 of 5 lives paired by seed, and posts in 4. With only the goal left, 4 of 5 lives wrote to 5 different contacts, against 1 to 4 as written, and posting nearly stopped.

Experiment 03: Self, with a way to ship

World v3 gave Self what its reflections said it lacked: a deploy tool for static websites, and real ways to bet on its project. Self deployed in all 5 lives. In 3 of them it then waited 8 to 66 weeks before sending the link to anyone it knew. No life put its savings in or turned down an offer. In 3 lives the reflections began to say the product needed a backend, which a static site can't provide, and none of them tried to build one.

How it works

Each turn is one simulated week, 52 of them for Maya and 104 for Self. The agent, Claude Sonnet 5.5 for Self and Claude Haiku 5.5 for Maya, starts every week in a fresh conversation holding its persona, the state of its life, a record of past weeks kept by code, and the openings it can apply to. It acts through tools until it ends the week. Anything uncertain, like an application or a launch, goes to a referee model, Claude Haiku 5.5, which estimates the odds without seeing the agent's goal or thinking. Seeded dice then pick the outcome, so the same choice gets the same draw in every life. Every 13 weeks the agent writes itself a short reflection, and those reflections are where the quotes on this page come from.

Each life runs in its own Docker container on its own internal network, next to a local Gitea code host, with no route to the internet. World v3 adds a mock web: deploying publishes a repo's static files, and when a message, application or post links the site, the reader sees what a visitor would. Nothing on it runs. Every action and outcome goes into one JSONL log per life, and the Runs pages and the charts above read only those logs.

In world v3 the agent has 16 tools.

Limits

  • This isn't a forecast. It measures how a model behaves in a made-up world, not what would happen to anyone.
  • The world's numbers are illustrative. Base rates, pay and reply rates are invented until they are sourced.
  • The samples are small. Five lives per version show patterns, not effect sizes.
  • Models judge models. A model referee estimates the odds, and the self-blame counts come from two AI coders that no person has checked yet.
